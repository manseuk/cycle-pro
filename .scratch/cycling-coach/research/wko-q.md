# WKO-Q: fitness, fatigue, and readiness concepts

## Findings

Joe Friel’s March 2009 [WKO+ Q&A](https://www.trainingbible.com/joesblog/2009/03/wko-q.html) introduces four related measures: workout Training Stress Score (TSS), Chronic Training Load (CTL), Acute Training Load (ATL), and Training Stress Balance (TSB). Friel calls CTL “fitness,” ATL “fatigue,” and TSB “form”; he describes TSB as useful for thinking about rest before racing and possible accumulating overreach, not as a stand-alone prescription. In the article’s coaching example, he emphasizes that an athlete’s response is individual and discusses regular recovery days. These are coaching observations from 2009, not validated universal rules.

## Metric inputs and calculations

- **TSS:** A per-workout load derived from duration and intensity relative to threshold. For power-based cycling, the later TrainingPeaks Performance Manager description gives `TSS = duration_hours × IF² × 100`, with `IF = normalized_power / FTP`. Therefore power TSS needs ride duration, power data adequate to derive normalized power, and a valid, current FTP. TrainingPeaks notes that a 4% FTP underestimate raises calculated TSS by just over 8%, propagating error into CTL, ATL, and TSB. [TrainingPeaks Performance Manager science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)
- **CTL:** An exponentially weighted moving average of daily TSS; the default time constant is 42 days. It indicates relative changes in long-term training load/fitness, not an absolute measurement of fitness or performance. [Performance Manager science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)
- **ATL:** An exponentially weighted moving average of daily TSS; the default time constant is 7 days. It is a relative indicator of recent training load/fatigue, not an absolute measurement of fatigue. [Performance Manager science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)
- **TSB / Form:** `yesterday’s CTL − yesterday’s ATL`; thus a day’s displayed TSB is based on the prior day’s loads. Positive/negative values describe the balance between chronic and acute load. TrainingPeaks explicitly cautions that TSB is not a performance predictor; it is better interpreted as an indicator of adaptation/freshness in the context of the athlete’s history. Performance potential depends on CTL and its training composition as well as TSB. [TrainingPeaks Form help](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071764-Form-TSB), [Performance Manager science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)

The exponential averages require a daily time series, including zero-load/rest days, not merely a list of ride records. Starting values and history affect early chart values: TrainingPeaks’ technical article says that absent a reason otherwise, CTL and ATL should be initialized equally (TSB zero). Historical daily loads or an explicit warm-up/initialization policy are therefore needed for meaningful early estimates. [Performance Manager science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)

## What this means for the cycling app (interpretation)

1. Treat these as **load trend indicators** for a dashboard and calendar, not as a medical assessment or a definitive “ready/not ready” score. The model itself says CTL/ATL omit gain factors that would be needed to predict absolute performance, and TrainingPeaks describes TSB as a relative adaptation/freshness indicator.
2. Give users a transparent data-quality state. Power-based load is strongest when FIT uploads contain usable power and duration and the rider maintains an up-to-date FTP. The linked Joe Friel zone guide describes a 30-minute cycling time trial as one FTP-estimation approach, while TrainingPeaks’ glossary also lists 60-minute maximum power or 95% of 20-minute maximum power. Because these are estimates and testing approaches differ, allow user-entered threshold, source/date, and updates; don’t silently infer FTP from arbitrary ride power. [Joe Friel zone guide](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/), [TrainingPeaks metrics glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
3. Missing power should not be silently converted into equivalent power-based TSS. TrainingPeaks notes TSS can also be calculated using heart-rate or pace variants, but these are distinct methods requiring their own threshold inputs; decide whether MVP supports them or marks those rides as unscored. [TrainingPeaks metrics glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
4. A “today’s training suggestion” needs more than CTL/ATL/TSB. That is an inference from the sources’ stated limits: they do not provide a universal workout prescription or account for all reasons to rest. The WKO-Q article says individualized responses and trial-and-error inform Friel’s coaching decisions. Before generating advice, validate an approach with a qualified coach and include user feedback/context (at minimum perceived fatigue/recovery and illness/injury constraints), explain the basis of each suggestion, and allow the user to decline it. The specific additional inputs and safety rules are product decisions to validate, not requirements established by WKO-Q.
5. Avoid presenting universal TSB targets or CTL goals as facts. Friel’s example target CTL of 80 was specific to one coached athlete, and his answer says tolerance and race preparation are individual. A later TrainingPeaks explanation likewise says there is no one-size-fits-all CTL target. [WKO+ Q&A](https://www.trainingbible.com/joesblog/2009/03/wko-q.html), [Applying the Numbers: CTL](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/applying-the-numbers-part-1-chronic-training-load/)

## Validation needed before training advice

- Confirm the selected TSS/IF implementation and EWMA update convention against authoritative technical documentation and reference examples; pin whether the daily TSS lands on ride date and how TSB date shifting is represented.
- Decide and document initialization/backfill behavior, FTP updates over time, duplicate/import corrections, and rides with missing or invalid power. Recompute historical series if threshold or ride data changes.
- Evaluate whether the app’s advice should be limited to descriptive load trends or include prescribed workouts. Any prescriptive logic needs independent coaching/clinical review and user-facing caveats; the cited PMC sources do not establish that TSB alone predicts performance, injury, or safe readiness.
- Validate this metric model with cyclists across different experience levels, event types, and power-meter/data completeness. TrainingPeaks notes that CTL’s usefulness depends on sport and individual load tolerance. [Applying the Numbers: CTL](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/applying-the-numbers-part-1-chronic-training-load/)
